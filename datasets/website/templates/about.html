About this site

A free, static reference to the international prizes that mark work with lasting impact, and to everyone who has received one. Every prize, every award year, and every recipient has its own page, and the pages are linked to each other by subject, country, and institution so that one name leads to the next.

Why it is useful to students

Award citations are the shortest honest summary of a discovery ever written. Each one states what was found, by whom, and why it mattered, in a sentence that had to survive a committee. This site collects them and makes them browsable in the terms a student already has.

  • Sorted by school subject. Every award is tagged with the class its work belongs to — Biology, Physics, Chemistry, Math, CS, History, Lit, Arts, Economics, Earth Science — so the subject pages turn "we are studying genetics this term" into the people who did the defining work in it.
  • Usable as a source. Every page carries a canonical address and links out to the awarding body's own record, so a citation in an essay or a bibliography is a copy and a paste rather than a search.
  • Fast and free everywhere. These are plain HTML pages. No account, no paywall, no cookie banner, no autoplaying anything. They open on a school Chromebook and on a phone with one bar of signal.
  • Practice at reading data. The explorer ranks laureates and countries and then lets a reader test the ranking — dividing by population changes the leaderboard completely, and noticing why is the lesson.
  • Geography and history in one view. The atlas plots where laureates were born against the institutions where they later worked. The distance between the two dots is a century of migration, visible at a glance.

What you can do here

  • Prizes — the award families, each with a short description and an editorial prestige score, opening onto that prize's categories, its award years, and a page for every recipient with the full citation.
  • People — every laureate on record, listed by surname. A person's page gathers all of their awards in one place, which is where the repeat winners become obvious.
  • Subjects — the same awards regrouped by high school class, with the institutions that recur in each field.
  • Countries — laureates by country of birth, award-time institution, or death, and Institutions — the universities, laboratories, and organizations where the recognized work was done.
  • Map — birthplaces and institutions on one atlas, filterable by subject.
  • Explorer — ranked leaderboards, laureates per million people, and a chart of those who won before turning forty.

Where the data comes from

One row per recipient, compiled from each award's official record and cross-checked against Wikipedia and Wikidata. Fields are verified individually rather than imported wholesale, and the standing rule is to leave a cell blank rather than guess: a missing birthplace here means nobody could confirm it, not that it was rounded to something plausible. Places use their present-day names, so a laureate born in Königsberg in 1904 is listed under Kaliningrad, Russia.

The pages themselves are generated from that database by a build script and served as fixed files. Nothing is assembled while you read it, and nothing about your visit is recorded. The one exception is the explorer, which may ask a public service which country you are browsing from in order to show your country's laureates first; if that request fails the rest of the page is unaffected.

What this site is not

The prestige score attached to each prize is an editorial judgement, not a measurement, and the points used to rank individuals follow from it — one Nobel counts as a full point, other awards a fraction of one. Reasonable people would score them differently.

The awards themselves carry a heavier bias than any ranking of them could. This set skews strongly toward the sciences, toward Europe and North America, and toward men, because the prizes did. The pages report that record as it stands; they do not correct it, and a name absent from this site is a statement about who has been recognized rather than about who did the work.
